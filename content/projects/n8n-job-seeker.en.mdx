The workflow was built on a free n8n account, which cannot export to JSON, so
what survives is node-level documentation rather than a runnable file. The
diagram above is drawn from that documentation — it describes the workflow as it
ran, not an idealised version of it.

## The threshold is the whole design

Stage one collects: an Apify actor scrapes LinkedIn search results into Google
Sheets, company names are aggregated and compared so a posting already in the
sheet is never appended twice.

Stage two scores. Each posting is put in front of a model together with the CV
and the LinkedIn profile, with a structured output parser forcing the answer
back into JSON so a compatibility score can be read out reliably instead of
parsed from prose.

Stage three writes — but only above **3.5 out of 5**. Below that, the posting
still gets its score written back to the sheet and the workflow stops there.

That gate is the point of the whole thing. An automation that generates a letter
for every posting it finds is not saving anyone effort, it is manufacturing
noise at a rate no human could match and pushing the cost onto whoever reads it.
The scoring step exists to throw work away.

## Where the human stays

The model scores and drafts; it never sends. Every generated letter lands in
Drive and is logged next to its posting in the sheet, which means the last step
before anything reaches an employer is a person reading what was written about
them.

That is a deliberate stopping point, not an unfinished feature. Sending is the
one step in this pipeline that cannot be taken back.

## What it shows

Orchestration across four services that were never designed to meet each other,
scoring with a structured contract rather than free text, and de-duplication
treated as a first-class concern instead of an afterthought — plus the judgement
to put a filter where the automation would otherwise scale in the wrong
direction.
