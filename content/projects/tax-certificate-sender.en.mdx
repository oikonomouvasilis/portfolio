Work for a public-sector body, on **suppliers' tax records**. The body is not
named here, and no company name, tax number or address appears. The screenshots
reproduce the shape of the reports with **fictional data**; the aggregate figures
are the real ones.

## The script that does not send

The main tool here is not the sender. It is `check_missing.py`, which does
exactly what the sender would do — read the tax number out of each PDF filename,
look it up in the register, validate the address — and then, instead of opening
an SMTP connection, writes a spreadsheet with one row per file and one of four
statuses: ready, bad filename, missing from the register, bad address.

The separation is not fastidiousness. A send cannot be recalled: the moment one
supplier's certificate reaches another, the mistake is permanent and it is a
disclosure of tax records. Running the whole matching logic first, with no
authority to send, turns "will this go to the right people?" into a file a
human can read before anything is opened.

## The 1,860 that were "missing"

The first report looked bad: **2,427 ready, 1,860 with no match in the
register** — nearly half the files. The obvious explanation was that the
22,256-row register was incomplete, and the obvious move was to ask for a new
one.

That was not it. The register had all of them.

The tax number in Excel is stored as a **number**, and a number has no leading
zeros. Of the 22,256 rows, **10,407 arrived eight digits long**, 31 at seven and
2 at six. The PDF filenames, by contrast, carried the tax number padded to nine.
`"099963235" == "99963235"` is `False`, so every supplier whose tax number starts
with a zero fell through the gap.

Of the 1,860 unmatched files, **1,859 had a tax number starting with zero**, and
**1,858 were found** the moment the register was padded back to nine digits. The
second run produced **4,284 ready and 8 left over** — three badly named files,
three invalid addresses, two genuinely absent.

That is the whole argument for the first script. The bug raised no exception,
logged no warning and was invisible in the code: 57% of the certificates would
simply have been sent, and the other 1,860 would have been chased down one phone
call at a time, for months.

## The send prefers to be interruptible

The sender does not try to finish in one run. After each successful send it
appends the filename to a log, and at the start of every run it skips whatever is
already in there. A batch limit and a three-second pause per message keep the
rate under the provider's ceiling.

The result is that an interruption — a dropped network, a closed laptop, a
provider limit — costs only the time up to that point. No run resends what has
already gone, and "where we left off" is a text file rather than a recollection.

## Two mistakes, and what became of them

I would not write these again — and in the repository they are no longer there.

The first is an `except: pass` around the send. It swallows every error
silently, so the log records what went out but never what failed or why. That
silence was paid for immediately afterwards: finding out what had not been
delivered meant downloading the mailbox itself and counting bounce messages —
work a proper log would have done for free.

The second is the account's app password, written into the file. It never made
it into a commit, but that was luck rather than design; it belonged in an
environment variable from the first line.

The published version fixes both: credentials are read from the environment, and
every failed send is written to `failed.log` with its reason. They are recorded
here because the first one has a visible consequence inside this very case study
— without it, the 276 bounces in the next section would not have had to be
counted out of a mailbox.

## What the bounces showed

Analysing two mailbox exports found **276 delivery failures** and 33 delays. By
SMTP reason: **248 non-existent mailboxes**, 11 blocked as spam, 5 full
mailboxes, 3 dead domains, 9 unclear.

The number is a lower bound rather than an exact total — it was measured on an
export of **unread** mail, so anything already opened was not counted. Even so,
roughly **one address in fifteen** in the register no longer leads anywhere.

The validation the first script performs is a check of **format**: that the text
looks like an address. No format tells you whether the mailbox still exists.
Those 276 were not a failure of the check; they were the register being older
than its suppliers — and they are the one list that, in hindsight, is worth
handing back to the body for correction.
