It grew out of a [spreadsheet](/en/projects/personal-expenses) that is still running.
The repository is private and stays that way: it runs on my own bank statements.
What follows is the engineering. The screenshots are from the running application
with the descriptions and amounts masked — no merchant, no figure and no category
breakdown from the underlying data appears anywhere on this site.

## Four banks, one schema

Piraeus, Alpha, Eurobank and the National Bank of Greece each export account
activity differently — different column names, different date formats, different
ideas about where the header row belongs, and in two cases a different file
format altogether.

The ingestion engine keeps one parser per bank and one target schema: date,
description, amount. Detection happens on the file's own shape rather than on
what the user says it is, because the person uploading a statement should not
have to know which of four buttons is the correct one.

Everything downstream — the classifier, the charts, the forecast — sees only the
target schema. Adding a fifth bank means writing a fifth parser and touching
nothing else.

## The model learns from being corrected

Categorisation is an MLP classifier over TF-IDF features of the description
text. That text is a mix of Greek and English, heavy on abbreviations, and it is
the only signal available — amounts say very little about what something was.

The design decision that matters is what happens **after** a prediction. Every
manual correction is written back to the database and mixed into the training
set, so the model is retrained from a static base plus the accumulated
corrections. It is not trained once and then slowly falsified by reality; it
gets more accurate the longer it is used, and specifically more accurate about
the merchants that this account actually sees.

Accuracy was measured, not estimated: **90.1% (±0.8%) under 3-fold
cross-validation, over 6,493 transactions in 32 categories**. The per-category
report stays private — the categories are somebody's spending habits.

## Forecasting is a different problem

Prediction and classification get confused with each other constantly, so they
are kept apart here. The classifier answers *what was this*. Prophet answers
*what does the next six months look like if the pattern holds*, working on the
time series of income and expenses rather than on individual rows.

Separating them means a bad forecast never corrupts the historical record, and
a re-categorisation never silently rewrites a projection that was already shown.
