This is where the [expenses work](/en/projects/expenses-predictor) started, and
it is still running. It is included because the spreadsheet is the honest first
version of the problem — and because it shows the ceiling that justified
building anything more complicated.

## Input, processing, output — kept apart

The workbook is six sheets and the split between them is the whole design.
`BUDGET TRACKING` is the only place anything is typed. `CALCULATIONS` holds the
intermediate work — the selected period, days elapsed, the savings rate.
`BUDGET DASHBOARD` only displays. `DROPDOWN DATA` feeds the validation lists.

Spreadsheets rot when those three jobs get mixed into the same cells: a total
typed over a formula, a chart pointed at a range that someone later sorted. The
separation is not elegance, it is the thing that lets a file survive three years
of daily use.

## The ledger refuses bad input

Entry is constrained rather than trusted. **Type** is a three-value list —
income, expenses, savings. **Category** is an `INDIRECT` dropdown that reads the
chosen type, so picking *Expenses* offers only expense categories. The date
column takes dates and the amount column takes numbers, and neither accepts
anything else.

That is why the dashboard can be believed. Every chart above it is an
aggregation, and an aggregation over dirty categories is a confident wrong
answer. Constraining the input at the point of entry is cheaper than cleaning it
afterwards, and it is the same argument the parsers in the ML app make later.

## The part it cannot do

One thing stays manual: choosing the category for each line. Over 1,179 records
that is 1,179 small decisions, and it is the only step that does not get faster
with practice.

That single unautomatable column is the reason the predictor exists. The
spreadsheet did not fail — it worked well enough, for long enough, to show
precisely which part of the job a model should take over.
