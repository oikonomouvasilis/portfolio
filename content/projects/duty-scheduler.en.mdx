Built for an organisation that runs on shifts. The repository is private and the
application is shown as a case study only: no unit, location, rank list or real
name appears here, and any screenshot comes from seeded fictional data.

## One table of facts

The data model is a single `assignments` table — who, which day, which duty —
and every statistic on every page is a query over it. Nothing is accumulated
into precomputed totals.

That is the decision the rest of the application rests on. Precomputed counters
are faster and they are also the standard way these systems start lying: someone
edits a day in the calendar, one of four counters does not get updated, and from
then on the roster and the fairness report tell different stories. Here they
read the same rows, so they cannot drift apart.

## What "fair" had to mean

Counting duties per person is the obvious measure and the wrong one. Two people
with the same count are not treated equally if one of them has been on the list
for three years and the other for three months, and a Sunday is not a Tuesday.

So the algorithm balances two quantities at once, both **normalised by each
person's available days since they started**: total duties, and heavy days —
weekends, public holidays and days flagged as special. On a heavy day, balancing
heavy load takes priority; on an ordinary day, total load does.

Hard constraints sit outside the optimisation entirely: declared
unavailabilities, which ranks a given duty is open to, people who have not
started yet, and never the same person twice on the same day.

## Greedy, then local search

Filling the month greedily gives a decent roster and an arbitrary one — early
choices constrain later days in ways nobody intended.

After the greedy pass, a deterministic local search moves automatic assignments
to other eligible people whenever that lowers the overall inequality. The
objective is the sum of squared normalised rates, which is minimised exactly
when those rates are equal; the two terms carry adjustable weights. Every
accepted move strictly decreases the objective, so the search terminates rather
than oscillating, and the same input always produces the same roster.

Manual assignments are never moved. The algorithm proposes; the person
responsible for the month decides, and the decision survives the next run.
